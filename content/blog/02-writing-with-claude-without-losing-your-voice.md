# Writing your book with Claude without letting it sound like Claude

Most authors who try writing with Claude watch their voice flatten out by chapter three. The prose starts smooth. It stays smooth. It never quite sounds like the writer reading aloud to a friend over coffee. By the time it is time to publish, the writer has a manuscript that reads like a chatbot working hard to sound human. That is the fear that keeps most Christian authors away from AI writing tools entirely.

There is a way through this. I have been writing alongside Claude for more than a year now on my own two forthcoming manuscripts, and I have worked out a method that keeps my voice intact across thousands of pages. I now teach it inside The Brave Purpose Author Method to writers who want AI in their workflow without letting it steamroll their voice. This essay is the compressed version of that method.

## What actually goes wrong

Claude does not have a voice of its own. It has a default cadence: mid-length sentences, tidy paragraph breaks, careful hedging, a fondness for the em dash, and a small vocabulary of soft transitional verbs (delve, leverage, unlock, elevate). None of these are inherently bad. But when they show up in every paragraph, the reader can feel it. The prose starts to read like nobody in particular wrote it.

The default cadence is what Claude does when you give it very little to work with. "Write a chapter about hope." "Draft the opening scene of a memoir." That kind of prompt gets you Claude's baseline. And Claude's baseline sounds like every other Claude output any reader has already seen.

The way through this is not better prompts. It is better source material.

## The voice-corpus method in one sentence

**Give Claude your own voice as the source material, then let it draft.**

Not a prompt engineering trick. A discipline about what Claude reads before it writes.

## Step 1: Build the corpus

For the first two to four weeks of any new book project, capture everything you say about the book, in your own voice, and turn it into text. Every phone call about the book. Every stray thought you dictate into your phone. Every conversation with your spouse about the chapter you are stuck on. Every prayer, every worry, every side comment. Wispr Flow or a similar dictation tool captures these in real time.

By the end of the first month, you should have between 15,000 and 40,000 words of your own voice, transcribed. Not written. Spoken. Your speaking voice is what your reader will hear on the page.

Some of that will be usable prose. Most of it will not. That is fine. The corpus is not the draft. The corpus is the voice signature.

## Step 2: Feed it to Claude at the start of every session

Before Claude drafts anything, it reads your corpus. The corpus becomes the "Project knowledge" or "system prompt" material in a Claude Project. When you ask Claude to draft chapter one, it drafts in the voice signature the corpus establishes.

The corpus grows over the engagement. As you dictate more session transcripts, more email drafts, more short essays, the corpus deepens. Claude gets a stronger signal every week.

## Step 3: Draft in your own voice first, always

Even with a strong corpus, Claude should never be the first draft of a chapter. You are. You dictate the opening scene of every chapter. You dictate the closing beat. You name the anecdote you want to tell. You mark where the chapter lands.

Claude's job in the first draft is to expand your dictated scaffold into a fully written chapter using your voice signature. Not to invent the chapter from scratch.

The order matters. When Claude expands what you already dictated, the draft sounds like a slightly polished version of you. When Claude drafts from a cold prompt, the draft sounds like Claude.

## Step 4: Edit like an editor

Every draft Claude returns gets an edit pass from you. You are looking for three things:

1. **Anything you would not say out loud.** Cross it out. Rewrite in the sentence you would actually say.
2. **AI-tell vocabulary.** The soft verbs (delve, leverage, unlock, elevate, seamless, robust, empower). Every one gets replaced with something concrete or cut entirely.
3. **Em dashes.** Cut them all. Replace with a period, a comma, a colon, or a full sentence break.

This edit pass takes 20-40 minutes per 4,000-word chapter once you build the muscle. It is the difference between a manuscript that sounds like you and one that sounds like it was written by a tool.

## Step 5: Read every chapter out loud before you ship it

The final voice pass. Read the chapter out loud. If a sentence catches you, rewrite it. If a paragraph feels dead, rewrite it. Your ear is the last line of defense against Claude's default cadence.

## What this looks like in practice

A writer I coach dictates for twenty minutes on Monday morning about the opening scene of chapter three. Wispr Flow saves the transcript. She uploads it to her Claude Project alongside the growing voice corpus. She asks Claude to expand her dictation into a full 4,000-word chapter draft. Claude returns a draft that sounds recognizably like her because it has read everything she has said about the book, everything she has said about her ministry, everything she has said in transcribed sermons, and her three prior chapter drafts.

She spends 30 minutes editing the draft. She cuts every em dash. She replaces two soft verbs with concrete ones. She reads the chapter aloud once. She ships it.

Elapsed time: less than two hours. Draft quality: publication-ready with the developmental-editor round still to come. Voice signature: intact.

That is what the voice-corpus method does.
