# How to write a Christian memoir when you have never written a book before

Most Christian memoirs never get written because the writer is stuck at the same starting line: I know I have a book in me and I have no idea what to do next. This guide answers that question. Not with a course. Not with a framework you have to master. With a small stack of moves that gets a real chapter onto a real page in a real week.

There was a four-year-old girl who used to hide in the safety of a dark closet. She felt unloved, uncertain, and lost. Slowly, through faith and the quiet resilience only time can reveal, she grew into a woman who cherishes life, believes she has worth, and knows what her dreams are for. That girl was me. And every book I have written has come out of the healing that started in that closet.

If a memory like that is what you have been carrying, this guide is for you.

## Start with your origin scene

Not the first scene of your life. The first scene of your book. The one small moment where the story you are about to tell actually begins.

For me it was a small group of new friends praying over me one afternoon when I was in my early forties. Not a big room. Not a stage. Five people around a coffee table in someone's living room. They shared a vision of Father God inviting me to dance with Him in the dance of life. I wept as I heard their words. That was the scene *Dancing with Father* opens with. Not because it was my earliest memory. Because it was the door I actually walked through.

Your origin scene has the same three qualities:

1. **It is small.** You can describe it in three sentences. If your origin scene requires two paragraphs of setup before the reader can enter, it is too big. Zoom in.
2. **It is sensory.** You can name what you were wearing, what the room smelled like, what somebody said out loud. Sensory detail is what turns a memory into a scene.
3. **You are inside it.** You are not narrating from above. You are in your own body, in the moment, with the emotional temperature the moment actually had.

Write the origin scene in one sitting. Two hundred words. Do not try to make it beautiful. Try to make it accurate.

## Build a voice corpus

The single most useful thing you can do in your first month is dictate everything. Every phone call about the book, every stray thought, every prayer, every conversation with your spouse, every response to a friend who asks how the writing is going. Dictate it into Wispr Flow or a similar tool. Save the transcripts.

That collection is your voice corpus.

Why does this matter? Because when you sit down to draft chapter one, you will not be writing from cold. You will be writing from your own already-spoken voice. And if you use AI tools like Claude or Gemini to help you draft (I do, and I coach my writers to do the same), the voice corpus is what makes the AI draft sound like you rather than like the AI's default cadence.

Voice corpus discipline in one line: **your book is already inside your speaking voice. Catch it before you edit it.**

## Name your reader

Not "Christian readers." Not "adults." One person. A specific reader, with a name and a life stage and a specific need.

For *Brave Purpose with God*, the reader I write to is a woman in her fifties who has spent thirty years teaching Sunday school, raising her children, and holding down a small business or a nonprofit board seat, and who has said out loud at least once, "I have a book in me and I do not know how to start." I know her. She has come up to me at every speaking engagement I have done in the last ten years. Her name changes. Her question does not.

Name your reader. Give her a face, a life stage, a scripture she keeps returning to, and the one question she keeps asking. Write your book to her.

## Choose a structure that holds

A memoir is not a diary. It is a shape.

The shape I have taught for the last decade and use in the coaching engagement I run is three Acts. Discover. Develop. Deliver. Four chapters per Act. Twelve chapters total. Around 4,000-4,500 words per chapter. Fifty to fifty-five thousand words for the whole book. This is the size a Christian living reader picks up and finishes.

- **Act I (Discover).** The reader meets you. Your origin. Your call. The cost you paid. The company you kept.
- **Act II (Develop).** The reader meets your framework. What you learned. What you practiced. What proved out. What the pattern was.
- **Act III (Deliver).** The reader is sent. The first step they take. The obstacles they will meet. The long haul. The benediction.

This structure is a scaffold. It is not a script. Your voice does the work inside the scaffold.

If you already have half a manuscript and you are wondering whether it fits, this is the exercise: map every existing scene against the three Acts and twelve chapters. What falls where? What is missing? What belongs somewhere else? You will find the answer in ninety minutes.

## Ship your first chapter

Not a perfect chapter. The chapter you can write in the next two weeks, using your origin scene, your voice corpus, your named reader, and the first slot of the twelve-chapter structure.

Draft it. Read it out loud. Ask one trusted reader to tell you honestly whether it sounds like you. Adjust. Ship.

Then start chapter two.
