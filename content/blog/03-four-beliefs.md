# The four beliefs that stop Christian authors from finishing their books

I have coached writers for a long time. Formally, informally, from a church platform, from a classroom, and now from a six-month coaching engagement I run out of my own consulting practice. And in all of it, four beliefs stop more Christian authors from finishing their books than any technical, structural, or publishing obstacle I can name.

The four beliefs are:

1. I cannot write.
2. Who am I to write this?
3. My story is not compelling.
4. I cannot write well enough.

I know these beliefs because I have coached writers through every one of them. I also know them because I have carried some of them myself. This essay names all four and shows the way through.

## Belief 1: I cannot write

The writer looks at the blank page and hears a voice inside them saying, "you are not a real writer." What that voice usually means is not "you do not know how to string sentences together." What it means is "you have never done this before, and doing new things is scary."

The way through this belief is not to prove the belief wrong. Trying to prove to yourself that you are a real writer produces exactly the kind of anxious prose readers can feel. The way through is to notice that "writer" is a category you are inventing to keep yourself out of the work. What you actually need to do is smaller. You need to dictate the opening scene of your book out loud. You need to describe one moment you cannot stop thinking about. You need to say ten sentences about your mother, or your calling, or the room where the story starts.

Those ten sentences are the first ten sentences of your book. You did not need to be a writer to say them. You needed to be honest and awake for a paragraph.

## Belief 2: Who am I to write this?

This belief has a religious edge that the first belief does not. The Christian author asks, "who am I to talk about God, or purpose, or healing? I am not a pastor, or a theologian, or a bestseller." The belief seems humble. It is not. It is a defense mechanism dressed in humility.

What sits underneath belief 2 is the assumption that the credential is what qualifies you to write. That the credential is what makes your book worth publishing.

The credential is not what qualifies you. What qualifies you is that you have lived something that another person in your same situation needs to hear. If you have walked through grief, or infertility, or church hurt, or a mid-life reckoning, or the raising of a child with a diagnosis, or the season your marriage almost ended, you have material. The material is the credential.

I was not a pastor when I wrote *Dancing with Father*. I was a woman who had lived through a specific healing. The book has reached women in Norway, Japan, and the Philippines, some of whom have written to tell me my little book kept them alive. None of them cared about my credential. They cared that I had walked their road first.

## Belief 3: My story is not compelling

This belief is a cousin of belief 2. It usually shows up when the writer starts to imagine a reader. "Who would care about my story? I did not overcome anything world-changing. I did not survive anything dramatic. I am a Sunday school teacher from Iowa. Why would anyone read my book?"

Every writer feels this. Every published author has felt this before their first book landed. The pattern shows up in every coaching engagement I run.

What makes a story compelling is not the size of the events. It is the depth of the honesty. A quiet story told with radical honesty lands harder than a dramatic story told carefully. Every reader who has ever thought "nobody else has felt this way" is waiting for a book that tells them somebody has. Your book is that book to some specific reader you have not met yet.

The way through belief 3 is not to have a more dramatic life. It is to write with a level of honesty that will make a specific reader say, "this is my story."

## Belief 4: I cannot write well enough

This belief is the most technically credible of the four. The writer looks at their draft and hears "this prose is not good enough." Sometimes they are right. Sometimes the prose is not good yet. The question is what to do about it.

Two failure modes at this stage:

- Some writers respond to "not good enough" by shelving the manuscript. They tell themselves they will come back to it when they are a better writer. They rarely do. The manuscript sits in the drawer.
- Other writers respond by outsourcing the work. They hire a ghostwriter, or they let an AI tool draft the whole book, or they hand the manuscript to a family member who "writes better than they do." The book gets finished. It does not sound like them. The reader can feel it.

There is a third response, and it is the one I teach. You accept that your first draft is not going to sound as good as you want it to. That is what first drafts are for. You develop a method for the second draft. You use AI tools if you want, and you use them as thinking partners. They pressure-test your logic. They do not write your sentences. You edit until every sentence sounds like you.

The book that comes out the other side is not perfect prose. It is your prose, at the strongest it has ever been. That is what a reader wants.

## The pattern under all four

All four beliefs share a common root. They are each a version of the sentence "I am not qualified for what I am about to do." That sentence is protecting you from the vulnerability of trying. It is not protecting you from failure. Failure is a book that never gets written. The four beliefs are the direct cause of failure.

The way through all four is to stop trying to be qualified and start trying to be honest. Say the ten sentences. Describe the one moment. Ship the first chapter. Trust that the reader who needs your book is a specific reader, waiting for a book only you can write.
